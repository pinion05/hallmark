Slow Web A manifesto in seven articles

The web can wait.

A manifesto for people who make pages — and the people who read them. Seven articles, one file, nothing that moves. This page is the argument and the demonstration.

Sign the manifesto Seven articles first, then a sentence to keep.

Somewhere between the feed and the notification, reading turned into grazing. Pages stopped ending. Sites began to behave like casinos with better typography — every scroll another pull of the lever, every visit measured in seconds wrung out of you. We are not against the web. We are against the hurry.

We want the other thing back: the page that is finished. The site that is one person’s shelf, dusted twice a year. The essay you close, and then think about at dinner. Slowness here is not nostalgia, and it is not lo-fi for its own sake — it is a decision about who the page serves. It serves the reader. Then it lets the reader go.

Article I

One page is enough.

A site can be a single page revised for years. Documents do not need growth strategies; they need care. Before adding a page, ask what the existing one would say about it.

Article II

Load once, then hold still.

Everything the reader needs arrives with the first response. No infinite scroll, no content that shuffles itself while being read. A finished page does not refresh itself.

Article III

Nothing moves unless the reader moves it.

No autoplay, no carousels, no paragraphs that fade in as if the writing were performing. Motion answers a reader’s gesture, or it does not happen. This page keeps that rule: nothing on it animates.

Article IV

Weigh less than you say.

Text is the densest medium the web has. A page should survive a weak signal in a stairwell — if the words arrive and nothing else does, the page should still work.

Article V

The reader sets the pace.

No countdowns, no streaks, no “only three left.” Urgency is a sales tool, not a reading aid. Whatever this page says today, it will still say next month — slightly better edited.

Article VI

Leave the door unlocked.

No interstitial asking for an email before the first sentence has been read. Come in, read, leave. Subscribing is something a reader decides at the end, on their own — or never.

Article VII

Publish less; keep it longer.

Revise instead of replace. Keep the URLs alive. A link that still works after ten years is a small public promise, kept — the web’s version of a stone bench.

Refusals & keepings

We refuse

  • Autoplay, in any form.
  • Infinite scroll.
  • Notification badges and their little red urgency.
  • Countdown timers on things that do not expire.
  • “Engagement” as a unit of worth.
  • Pop-ups that speak before the writing does.

We keep

  • RSS, faithfully.
  • Permalinks that outlive redesigns.
  • Alt text, written like it matters.
  • The back button, unbroken.
  • Margins wide enough to rest in.
  • The right to leave without being asked why.

How to sign

There is no form, and no list of names to scroll. Signing is something you do on your own site, in your own footer. Add the line below to your colophon, your about page, or the bottom of whatever you keep on the web — then live up to it.

This is a slow web page. It loads once, says its piece, and lets you go.

No account, no counter, no confirmation. We won’t know you signed. That is the point.
